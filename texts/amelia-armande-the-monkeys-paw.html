Amelia Armande - The Monkey’s Paw

On a cold, stormy evening, the White family welcomes an old friend who has brought back a strange souvenir from India: a monkey’s paw said to grant three wishes.

At first, they laugh at the story and treat it like a game. But when Mr White decides to make a simple wish, the family soon discovers that getting what you want may come at a terrible price…

Characters

Mr White – late 50s, retired businessman

Mrs White – late 40s, his wife

Herbert – mid 20s, their son

Morris – late 50s, a Sergeant-Major, who spent many years serving in India

Man – employee of Maw & Meggins, the factory where Herbert works

Scene 1

(Early 1900s. A small parlour with armchairs, a table with chairs, a piano, a window and the front door of the house with a chain, and a top and bottom bolt. Outside, a wet and windy evening, but inside is cosy and a fire burns in the hearth.)

(Mr White and his son Herbert are playing a game of chess. Herbert is winning. Mrs White sits knitting by the fire. Mr White moves a knight. Mrs White tuts.)

Mrs White: I wouldn’t move there if I was you.

Mr White: I know what I’m doing!

Mrs White: You’ll spot it in a minute.

Herbert: Spot what?

Mr White: Hush up, mother. What would you know about it?

(Herbert studies the chess board, looking for what his mother has seen. Mr White spots it first. He clears his throat and tries to distract Herbert.)

Mr White: Hark at the wind.

Herbert: (Not looking away.) I’m listening.

(Herbert moves a piece.)

Herbert: Check.

Mr White: (Reaching out and moving a piece.) I should hardly think that he’d come tonight.

Herbert: Mate.

(Mr White throws his King over.)

Mr White: That’s the worst thing - living so far out! Of all the beastly, slushy, out-of-the-way places to live in, this is the worst! Pathway’s a bog, and the road’s a torrent. I don’t know what people are thinking about. I suppose because only two houses in the road are let, they think it doesn’t matter!

Mrs White: Never mind, dear. Perhaps you’ll win the next one.

Mr White: I...!

(Mrs White and Herbert share a knowing glance, which Mr White spots. He smiles guiltily.)

(There is a knocking at the door.)

Herbert: There he is.

(Mr White opens the door to let Mr Morris in. He is a friend of the family, a soldier and well-travelled.)

Mr White: Morris! How are you?

Morris: White! Good to see you.

Mr White: Come in, come in. Come and meet the family.

Morris: Thank you. Awful night.

Mrs White: We hope you didn’t have any trouble.

Mr White: The road’s terrible when it rains.

Morris: It was no trouble at all. (To Herbert.) Hello Little Bertie!

Herbert: Mr Morris, I’m a grown man now.

Mrs White: Got himself a job at Maw and Meggins and everything.

Morris: Well, well! Quite the eligible bachelor, eh?

Mrs White: We’re very proud of him. Come through and dry off.

Morris: Thank you - I’m soaked.

Mr White: And tell us all about your travels. I’ve been telling the others, but you do it much better than me. (He brings him a drink.)

Mrs White: The dark wilds of India! How exciting!

(They all start to move off to another room.)

Morris: Well now, let’s see... have you heard about the strange people whose heads grow beneath their shoulders?

(They leave in a flurry of excitement.)

(Blackout)

(A sense of time having passed. It is later in the evening. Morris and the family enter, merry from a few drinks.)

Morris: And I tell you, by my life, it got up and danced the polka before our very eyes! Well, as you can imagine, we crossed ourselves and ran out of there as fast as our legs could carry us!

(They seat themselves, laughing.)

Mr White: Sounds like a real adventure the way you tell it. I’d like to go to India myself. Just to look round a bit, you know.

Morris: Better where you are. (He drinks.)

Mr White: I should like to see all those old temples and fakirs and jugglers. And what was that you started telling me the other day about a monkey’s paw or something, Morris?

Morris: (Quickly) Nothing. Leastways, nothing worth hearing.

Mrs White: Monkey’s paw?

Morris: Well, it’s just a bit of what you might call, magic, perhaps.

(They sit up, interested. Mr White refills Morris’ glass. He fumbles in his pocket.)

Morris: To look at, it’s just an ordinary little paw, dried to a mummy.

(He takes a withered monkey paw out of his pocket and holds it out to Mrs White, who draws back with a grimace. Herbert takes it and studies it curiously.)

Mr White: (Taking it from Herbert.) And what is there special about it? (He puts it down.)

Morris: It had a spell put on it by an old fakir – a very holy man. He wanted to show that fate ruled people’s lives, and that those who interfered with it - did so to their sorrow. He put a spell on it so that three separate people could each have three wishes from it.

(The others laugh, but Morris is very serious.)

Herbert: Well, why don’t you have three, sir?

Morris: (Quietly) I did.

Mrs White: And did you really have the three wishes granted?

Morris: I did. (He drinks.)

Mrs White: Has anyone else wished?

Morris: The first man had his three wishes, yes. I don’t know what the first two were for, but the third was for his death. That’s how I got the paw.

(A hush falls on the group.)

Mr White: If you’ve had your three wishes, it’s no good to you now then, Morris. What do you keep it for?

Morris: Fancy, I suppose. I did have some idea of selling it, but I don’t think I will. It has caused enough mischief already. Besides, people won’t buy. They think it’s a fairy tale; some of them, and those who do think anything of it want to try it first and pay me afterwards.

(Mrs White leaves the room to get more drink.)

Mr White: (Picking up the monkey paw.) How do you do it?

Morris: Hold it up in your right hand and wish aloud. (He takes it off him.) But I warn you of the consequences.

Mr White: If you could have another three wishes, would you have them?

Morris: I don’t know. I don’t know.

(He holds it, dangling it between forefinger and thumb, and suddenly throws it on the fire. Mr White cries out and snatches it off.)

Morris: Better let it burn.

Mr White: If you don’t want it, Morris, give it to me.

Morris: I won’t. I threw it on the fire. If you keep it, don’t blame me for what happens. Pitch it on the fire again like a sensible man.

(Mr White tucks it in his pocket. Mrs White comes back on with the whiskey.)

Mrs White: All these wishes, sounds like the Arabian Nights. Don’t you think you might wish for four pairs of hands for me?

(Mr White takes the monkey paw out of his pocket as if to make the wish. With a look of alarm, Morris catches his arm. All but Morris laugh.)

Morris: If you must wish, wish for something sensible.

Mr White: Alright, alright. (He puts it back in his pocket.)

Morris: I’d best be going.

Mrs White: You won’t stay for supper?

Morris: I must catch the last train, I’m afraid.

Mr White: Well, you must let me give you something for this. (He pats his pocket.)

Morris: No, I said, I didn’t give it you.

Mr White: I can’t just take it for nothing – here. (He presses a note into his hand.)

Morris: I’d rather...

Mr White: I insist. (He looks at him for a moment.)

Morris: Throw it away, Jacob. Don’t keep it. (He leaves.)

Mrs White: Very strange. Why would you want to throw away something so valuable?

(Herbert pulls the monkey paw out of his father’s pocket.)

Herbert: If the tale about the monkey paw is not more truthful than those he has been telling us, we shan’t make much out of it. (He laughs.) Here, wish to be an emperor, father, to begin with; then you can’t be henpecked.

Mrs White: Hey!

(He throws it to his father as Mrs White pursues him armed with an antimacassar.)

Mr White: I don’t know what to wish for, and that’s a fact. It seems to me I’ve got all I want.

Herbert: If you only paid off the house, you’d be quite happy, wouldn’t you? Well, wish for two hundred pounds then. That’ll just do it.

(Mr White smiles shamefacedly and holds the paw up in his right hand. Herbert sits at the piano in mock solemnity, winking at his mother, and strikes a few ominous chords.)

Mr White: I wish for two hundred pounds.

(Herbert crashes a chord on the piano. Mr White cries out and drops the monkey paw. Herbert and Mrs White both move to him.)

Mr White: It moved. As I wished, it twisted in my hand like a snake.

Herbert: (Picking up the paw and putting it on the table.) Well I don’t see the money. And I bet I never shall.

Mrs White: It must have been your fancy, father.

(Mr White shakes his head.)

Mr White: Never mind, though. There’s no harm done, but it gave me a shock all the same.

(They sit.)

Herbert: I expect you’ll find the cash tied up in a big bag in the middle of your bed, and something horrible squatting up on top of the wardrobe, watching you as you pocket your ill-gotten gains.

(They laugh a little - and then all start as the wind blows a shutter open.)

Mr White: Come on, let’s go to bed.

(All exit. Blackout.)

Scene 2

(The next afternoon. Mrs White is serving Mr White tea as he reads the paper. Mr White is in a well-worn gardening coat and boots.)

Mrs White: The idea of us listening to such nonsense! How could wishes be granted in these days? And if they could, how could two hundred pounds hurt you, father?

Mr White: Might drop on my head from the sky.

(They laugh.)

Mr White: Morris said these things happen so naturally that you could, if you wished, attribute it to coincidence.

Mrs White: Well, there was nothing in the post but a tailor’s bill. Herbert said not to break into the money before you got back from work. He’s worried it will turn you into a mean, avaricious man, and then we shall have to disown you. (She laughs.) He’ll have more funny remarks, I expect, when he comes home.

Mr White: I dare say. But for all that, the thing moved in my hand; that I’ll swear to.

Mrs White: You thought it did.

Mr White: I say it did, there was no thought about it; I had just – what’s the matter?

(Mrs White is staring out of the window.)

Mrs White: There’s a man outside. He’s passed by three times now and each time looked in, like he’s trying to make up his mind whether to enter. (She stands, untying her apron.) He’s coming down the path.

(A knocking at the door. Mrs White goes and answers the door. The Man is smartly dressed in formal, dark clothes and top hat. He stands anxiously in the doorway.)

Mrs White: Can we help you?

Man: Mr and Mrs White?

Mrs White: Yes. Come in. Sorry about the room. My husband’s just come in from the gardening, we weren’t expecting guests.

Man: I – was asked to call. (He stoops and picks something off his trouser leg.) I come from Maw and Meggins.

(Mrs White starts.)

Mrs White: Is anything the matter? Has anything happened to Herbert? What is it? What is it?

Mr White: (Hastily) There, there, mother. Sit down, and don’t jump to conclusions. You’ve not brought bad news, sir, I’m sure.

Man: I’m sorry...

Mrs White: (Wildly) Is he hurt?

Man: (Nods) Badly hurt. But he is not in any pain.

Mrs White: Oh, thank God! Thank God for that! Thank...

(She stops as the implications of the assurance dawn on her, and looking at the Man’s tortured face confirms her fears. She gasps, turns to her husband, who is staring blankly, and puts her trembling hand on his.)

Man: He was... caught in the machinery.

Mr White: (Dazed) Caught in the machinery. Yes. (He puts his other hand on top of his wife’s and squeezes it. He turns to the Man.) He was the only one left to us. It is hard.

Man: (Looking away.) The firm wished me to convey their sincere sympathies with you in your great loss. I beg that you will understand I am only their servant and merely obeying orders. (Pause) I was to say that Maw and Meggins disclaim all responsibility. They admit no liability at all, but in consideration of your son’s services, they wish to present you with a certain sum as compensation.

(Mr White drops his wife’s hand and rises to his feet, a look of horror on his face.)

Mr White: How much?

Man: Two hundred pounds.

(Mrs White shrieks. Mr White smiles faintly, and passes out on the floor. Blackout.)

Scene 3

(Ten days later. Deep night. The fire is dying in the grate. Mr White sleeps in the armchair. By the window, Mrs White weeps. The noise of her crying wakens Mr White.)

Mr White: (Tenderly) Go to bed. You will be cold.

Mrs White: It is colder for my son.

(Too tired to argue, Mr White lies back in the chair and closes his eyes. Mrs White weeps to herself. Suddenly, she stops. A thought has struck her.)

Mrs White: Oh! (She staggers away from the window, shaking Mr White by the shoulder.) The paw! The monkey’s paw!

Mr White: (Startled awake.) Where? Where is it? What’s the matter?

Mrs White: You’ve not destroyed it?

Mr White: It’s in the parlour on the bracket. Why?

(She cries and laughs together, kissing his cheek.)

Mrs White: I only just thought of it! Why didn’t I think of it before? Why didn’t you think of it?

Mr White: Think of what?

Mrs White: The other two wishes. We’ve only had one.

Mr White: (Fiercely) Was not that enough?

Mrs White: No - we’ll have one more. Go down and get it quickly, and wish our boy alive again.

(Mr White stares at her in horror.)

Mr White: Good God, you are mad!

Mrs White: Get it! Get it quickly and wish – Oh, my boy, my boy!

Mr White: Get to bed. You don’t know what you are saying.

Mrs White: We had the first wish granted; why not the second?

Mr White: A coincidence!

Mrs White: (Excited) Go and get it and wish.

Mr White: (His voice shaking.) He has been dead ten days, and besides he – I would not tell you else, but – I could only recognise him by his clothing. If he was too terrible for you to see then, how now?

Mrs White: (Dragging him off.) Bring him back! Do you think I fear the child I have nursed?

(He goes and gets it, coming back trembling. Mrs White stares at him fearsomely.)

Mrs White: (In a strong voice.) Wish!

Mr White: It is foolish and wicked.

Mrs White: Wish!

(Mr White raises the monkey paw in his right hand.)

Mr White: I wish my son alive.

(The monkey paw falls to the floor. Trembling, Mr White seats himself. Mrs White goes to the window and watches. The clock chimes one.)

(A sense of time having passed. The clock chimes three. Mrs White has fallen asleep at the window. Mr White breathes a sigh of relief and carries Mrs White off up to bed. He comes back and picks up the monkey paw.)

(Tiny, but audible, a knocking is heard at the front door.)

(Mr White gasps, throwing the monkey paw away from him, drawing back from the door.)

(The knock is sounded again.)

Mrs White: (Enters) What’s wrong?

Mr White: A rat, a rat. It ran across the room.

(The knocking sounds again.)

Mrs White: What’s that? (The knocking sounds again, louder.)

Mrs White: It’s Herbert!

(She runs for the door, but Mr White blocks her way, grabbing her arms.)

Mr White: (A hoarse whisper.) What are you going to do?

Mrs White: (Struggling) It’s my boy; it’s Herbert! I forgot the cemetery was two miles away. What are you holding me for? Let go. I must open the door.

Mr White: For God’s sake, don’t let it in!

(The knocking sounds again, louder.)

Mrs White: (Struggling) You’re afraid of your own son! Let me go! I’m coming, Herbert! I’m coming!

(The knocking sounds again, louder and more insistent. Mrs White breaks free of Mr White with a sudden wrench and runs for the door. She pulls the chain back and struggles with the stiff bottom bolt, which is slowly drawn back. All the while the knocking sounds, louder and louder.)

(Mr White has dropped to his hands and knees, searching in panic for the monkey paw.)

(Mrs White reaches for the top bolt. It is too high for her.)

Mrs White: The bolt. Help. I can’t reach it.

(The knocking continues, booming through the house. Mr White ignores her, breathing hard in his panic. Mrs White drags a chair over to the door, climbs on it and starts working the bolt loose.)

(At this moment, Mr White finds the monkey paw. He holds it over his head and screams over the knocking.)

Mr White: Send it back! Make it dead again!

(The knocking ceases. Mrs White pulls back the chair and opens the door. Outside, there is no one. She sinks to the floor with a wail of disappointment and misery. Mr White relaxes, breathing hard, weeping. He collects her up and closes the door.)

(Curtain)
